How this example works

Monthly commute hours = one-way minutes × 2 × commute days ÷ 60. Monthly distance = one-way miles × 2 × commute days. Monthly fuel cost = monthly distance ÷ miles per gallon × fuel price per gallon.

The comparison adds 15 minutes to each one-way trip. It does not assume more miles or assign a dollar value to time. Presets change only the route: the shorter example uses 15 minutes and 10 miles; the longer example uses 35 minutes and 25 miles. Your schedule and fuel assumptions stay as entered.

The model assumes one round trip per commute day in a gasoline or diesel vehicle. It does not model electric charging, carpooling, public transit, or extra daily trips. Values are rounded for display.

Turn a long wish list into a clearer housing search. Separate the essentials from the preferences, then compare homes against the needs of your household.

Keep the essentials clear.What must a home provide for your life to work?Tap to look closer

Start with practical needs such as an accessible layout, manageable costs, or a workable route to duty. A true requirement should be specific enough to check.

Name your preferences.What would improve the fit without deciding it?Tap to look closer

Extra storage, a newer kitchen, or outdoor space may matter without being essential. Keep preferences visible so you can weigh them after the basics are met.

Choose where to flex.Decide which compromises you could live with.Tap to look closer

Flexibility means you are open to alternatives—not that something has no value. Compare the effect on your time, costs, and daily routine before changing a requirement.

Financial Guidance / 03 · Understand your budget

See what goes out.
Know what stays available.

Bring income, housing, everyday costs, and savings into one monthly picture. Explore how a housing choice could affect the money left for everything else.

Start with the full picture ↓ Explore a monthly budget ↓ Try a change ↓
Use the income you receive.Start with take-home pay, not gross pay.Tap to look closer

Use a consistent monthly take-home amount from your pay and bank records. If BAH or other allowances are already included in your deposits, do not add them again. Do not subtract payroll deductions a second time.

Count the whole housing cost.The payment alone does not tell the full story.Tap to look closer

Include the costs that apply to the home: payment or rent, utilities, insurance, taxes, fees, and upkeep. If taxes and insurance are already in a mortgage payment, do not add them again.

Plan beyond the regular bills.Give savings and occasional costs a place.Tap to look closer

Include savings you plan to set aside from take-home income and a monthly amount for irregular expenses. Keep moving costs, deposits, closing funds, and other upfront needs on a separate list.

Adjust savings & irregular costs

Annual irregular costs are divided by 12. Examples: annual bills or seasonal spending not counted elsewhere. Savings and irregular-cost reserves are separate allocations; avoid counting either twice.

Defaults include $500/month of savings and $4,200/year of irregular costs ($350/month). Savings already deducted from your pay are not added here.

How this example is calculated

Monthly irregular-cost reserve = annual irregular expenses ÷ 12. Total allocated = housing + everyday spending + debt payments + planned savings + irregular-cost reserve. Remaining = take-home income − total allocated.

A negative result is a gap between income and the entered plan. A positive result is unallocated money in this model—not a guarantee that a home is affordable. Calculations use full precision; displayed amounts round to cents.

This is a monthly average. Pay dates, bill timing, reimbursements, changes in allowances, and one-time moving costs are not modeled. Use your own records to check those separately.
